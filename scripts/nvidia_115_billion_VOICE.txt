On Tuesday evening, Nvidia told the world it had just made one hundred and fifteen point eight billion dollars in three months. That's more than double what it made a year earlier. It beat every forecast on Wall Street. It promised an even bigger quarter next. For a few hours, the stock went up. Then the sun came up on Wednesday, and it fell. Not a crash. Just under three percent. But it was the kind of fall that tells you something. Because this was the first time in thirty-three years that Nvidia reported its results without Jensen Huang. And the market had a question that no number on that page could answer. This is the story of the best quarter Nvidia has ever had, and why it still wasn't enough.

Let's start with what Nvidia actually reported. Revenue: one hundred and fifteen point eight billion dollars. Up one hundred and three percent from a year ago. Wall Street had expected about one hundred and nine. Almost all of it, one hundred and seven point six billion, came from one place: data centers. The giant buildings full of chips that train and run artificial intelligence. Profit per share beat too. Analysts wanted about two dollars and fifty cents. Nvidia delivered two sixty-eight. And for every dollar of chips it sold, it kept roughly seventy-four cents after making them. Most companies would frame that number and hang it in the lobby. Then came the forecast. For the next quarter, Nvidia said to expect about one hundred and thirty billion dollars. Analysts had pencilled in around one hundred and twenty-five. And it said its brand-new chips, called Vera Rubin, were already shipping to customers. In the same quarter, it handed twenty-eight point one billion dollars back to its shareholders. On paper, there was nothing to fix. Imagine a student who scores a hundred and three percent on a test. Then asks for extra homework. And gets a raise in their allowance. That was Nvidia on Tuesday night.

Here's what was missing from the call. The man who usually opens it. On the twelfth of November, Jensen Huang resigned as president, chief executive and board member, effective immediately. Five days later, the person reading the numbers was Colette Kress. Nvidia's chief financial officer for more than a decade. Now also its interim chief executive. Two jobs, one microphone. She did what a finance chief does best. She was calm, precise, and boring in the best possible way. The company, she said, was moving forward. The next morning, she held her first all-hands meeting with Nvidia's staff. And she gave them one piece of very concrete good news. The yearly pay review was moving up, from March to December. That's not a strategy. That's a hug. But here's the problem with replacing a founder with a spreadsheet. Investors don't only buy what a company earns this quarter. They buy what they believe it will earn in ten years. And for thirty-three years, the person who told them what Nvidia would become was Jensen. He picked the bets. He sold them on stage. In a black leather jacket, he was the forecast. On Tuesday, the forecast had numbers. It didn't have a face.

So why does a company beat every estimate and still go down? There are three reasons, and none of them are on the income statement. The first one is the oldest rule on Wall Street. A stock price is not a grade for the past. It's a bet on the future. And by Tuesday, everybody already expected Nvidia to be great. Think of a restaurant that's famous for its steak. If the steak is perfect, nobody cheers. That's just Tuesday. The only thing that can move the price is a surprise. And a beat that everyone sees coming isn't one. The second reason is China. Nvidia's forecast for the next quarter assumes zero data center chip sales to China. None. The biggest market for artificial intelligence outside America has been written down to nothing. Not because the demand disappeared. Because Washington says no. And after the last two weeks, this is not a company that wants to argue with Washington. The third reason is smaller, but analysts noticed it. Those beautiful margins? Nvidia said they'll dip next quarter to around seventy-one or seventy-two cents on the dollar, before they recover. New chips cost more to build at first. It's still a number most companies would kill for. But when a stock is priced for perfection, a little less perfect is a reason to sell. Put those three together, add a founder who just walked out the door, and you get the strangest chart of the year. The company kept growing. The stock kept shrinking. By Wednesday's close, Nvidia was down about twenty-six percent in November alone. And roughly a third below its record in early October.

And this wasn't just about one company. Nvidia is the thermometer of the whole artificial intelligence boom. When it shivers, everyone checks their temperature. The same week, a monthly survey of big fund managers by Bank of America reported the largest one-month cut to technology holdings since two thousand and six. In Taiwan, where most of the world's advanced chips are made, the stock market fell more than four percent in a day. The companies that rent out Nvidia's chips to everyone else fell with it. And then, on Thursday, the biggest artificial intelligence company yet to go public, Anthropic, started trading on the Nasdaq. So in one week, the market was being asked two questions at the same time. Is artificial intelligence still the future? And how much should anyone pay for it today? The first answer was yes. The second answer was "less than last month."

You might not own a single share of Nvidia. But if you have a retirement account that tracks the market, you probably do. Nvidia is one of the largest companies in the S and P five hundred. When it falls a quarter of its value in a month, a small piece of your savings falls with it. Here's the twist. After this drop, Nvidia's price compared to its profits is the lowest it's been in about ten years. One way to read that: the stock is on sale. Another way: the market is charging a discount for uncertainty. A company without its founder. A country it can't sell to. A boom that some people think is getting old. Both readings can be true at the same time. That's what makes it a market. This isn't advice to buy or sell anything. It's a reminder of what you actually own when you own a fund. You don't just own the profits. You own the mood.

So let's go back to Tuesday night. One hundred and fifteen point eight billion dollars. Up one hundred and three percent. A forecast of one hundred and thirty billion more. Every number Nvidia could control, it delivered. The one number it couldn't put on the page was the only one investors were looking for. How much is this company worth without the man who built it? Nobody knows yet. Not the board. Not the analysts. Not Colette Kress. The chips didn't change. The factories didn't stop. The customers didn't cancel. Nvidia proved this week that it can still make money without Jensen Huang. What it hasn't proved yet is that it can still make people believe. And on Wall Street, belief is the only product that's always sold out.
